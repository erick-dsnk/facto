# Ranking for objective "challenge-prize": probability of winning the partner award

**Summary**
- The best partner-prize bet is **Trusted Trade Agents (Valio Aimo), at 75**. It is pure software and its rubric rewards exactly what we build well: an end-to-end agent flow (30%), guardrails and audit (20%) and exception handling (15%) (CHALLENGES.md:L659-679). The obvious entries are wide but shallow. The slugs `valio-aimo` and `trusted-trade-agents` are **the same challenge** (the "Valio Aimo®" heading at CHALLENGES.md:L575 is a subsection of Trusted Trade Agents, L535), so both get the same score.
- The next tier sits within about five points, which is inside the error of our competition estimates: **Decode the Noise (72)**, **Wikipedia Through Time (71)**, **Make Blueprints Talk (70)**, **Earned Trust in AI Code (67)** and **Break the Agent (67)**.
- The physical-robotics challenges (Intel, Rokoko, Aalto humanoid, Arduino) rank at the bottom. Smaller fields help them, but robot time, kit access and live-hardware demo risk outweigh that for this team.
- Every competition-density score is an **estimate**: no source gives the number of teams per challenge. The one exception is Spread, which had about 64 teams registered at level 8 on Friday evening (spread/feasibility.md).

All scores are my estimates. They draw on the research reports under `02-research/` and the analyst files under `01-challenge-intelligence/`, and the challenge facts they cite are given as line references.

## Criteria and weights

Each criterion is scored 0-10, multiplied by its weight and summed to a 0-100 score. Overall-jury appeal is ignored, as the objective requires.

| Criterion | Weight | What a 10 means |
|---|---|---|
| **Fit**: sponsor wants what we can build exceptionally well | 25% | The rubric rewards full-stack/agent/ML/mobile depth; no reliance on embedded or robotics skills |
| **Density** (inverse): competition from Junction teams | 20% | Few teams expected, or a hard filter (gate, difficulty, limited kits) thins the field. Estimates only |
| **Differentiation**: room to beat the obvious entries | 20% | Clear, evidenced gaps that the obvious builds miss (taken from the opportunity reports and re-checked) |
| **Feasibility and demo reliability** (includes the hardware penalty) | 20% | Buildable well within 40 h, with a deterministic and rehearsable demo. Penalised in proportion to physical work and to dependence on shared kits |
| **Showcase**: how well an entry can show off the sponsor's technology | 15% | The sponsor's tech or data is central and visibly exercised |

Notes on calibration:
- The research agents' 1-10 numbers were used as inputs only. Where a placement depended on them I re-checked the reports: Spread's top-10 gate, the competitive reports for Earned Trust and Valio, and the duplicated Valio entry.
- Prize size is **not** a criterion, because the objective is probability, not expected value. A larger prize may, however, attract more teams, and that is folded into Density.
- "Win" means first place. Most challenges also have 2nd and 3rd prizes, so podium odds are higher everywhere except AI Glasses, which has a single winner (CHALLENGES.md:L943).

## Top six in detail

### 1. Trusted Trade Agents / Valio Aimo (75)
This challenge rewards the shape of system we build fastest: an agent, a deterministic core, a policy engine, an approval UI and an audit log, all graded against a concrete mocked environment (L663). The research agents verified the price and availability mock live and found it fast and deterministic (trusted-trade-agents/feasibility.md, valio-aimo/feasibility.md). The data has measurable traps that obvious "MCP plus chatbot" entries will miss: unsafe substitution pairs, sentinel stock values, passed cut-offs, and lactose-free versus the milk allergen. That is a ready-made story for robustness (15%) and controls (20%). Density is the soft spot. The competitive report estimates the field as "wide but shallow" with no sourced team counts, and €4k is the largest cash prize in the Agentic track, which may pull teams. **What would change it:** an on-site count of more than about 15 teams on Valio, or Valio announcing an order endpoint or format that commoditises our order layer, would drop it to the 70 tier. Confirmation that judges run a hosted flow and value AP2/UCP-style openness would raise it.

### 2. Decode the Noise (72)
Detection quality is 40% of the score and must be measured: precision, false-positive rate, explainability (L76-79). That favours an ML team over the many LLM-only "is this propaganda" wrappers. The mandatory live Hostinger deployment (L29) is easy for us and eliminates weaker teams. Closing the loop through the VPS firewall API is a differentiator that also shows off Hostinger, the sponsor's real goal. It ranks below Valio because the sponsor's technology is infrastructure (a VPS and an AI Router) rather than a domain API. The judges may also treat web-traffic bot detection as less on-theme than propaganda, which is unknown. **What would change it:** if Hostinger mentors confirm that traffic and bot triage is squarely in scope and that Router credits are ample, it moves level with Valio. Tiny credits, or a strong preference for text propaganda (where the PTC corpus is academic-use only), would lower it.

### 3. Wikipedia Through Time (71)
MariaDB use is scored explicitly (20%), and the sponsor's own starters leave combining RAG with time travel as a "later step". A time-aware RAG with revision-level citations is therefore both differentiated and a strong showcase, earning the top Showcase score of 9. The €1,000 prize (L1934) probably keeps the field moderate (estimate). The feasibility report rates a minimum version at about 95% and a strong combined version at 70-80%. The slip risks are loading 862k revisions into system-versioned tables and embedding dataset L, and scalability is worth 20%. **What would change it:** mentors saying an M subset plus extrapolated numbers satisfies scalability raises it. Discovering that a vector index cannot coexist with system-versioned tables at all (undocumented) or many RAG teams on site lowers it.

### 4. Make Blueprints Talk (70)
This is the most ML-heavy software challenge and plays to the team's data-science strength. The feasibility spike already got exact answers from IFC and DXF, about 33% of public questions are deterministic with true-by-construction evidence, and the difficulty (rated 7) deters generic chat-with-PDF teams. Two weaknesses hold it back. First, 67% of questions are CubiCasa raster counts, where VLMs score about 0.40-0.55 and a trained detector carries a possible leakage question. Second, the accuracy and cost evaluation protocol is unknown (L1607-1610), so we cannot be sure our strengths are what gets measured. **What would change it:** if Stoa confirms a hidden test set weighted towards vector and IFC sources, or allows training on the CubiCasa annotations, it could take first place. A raster-only hidden set with tight cost scoring would drop it to the mid-60s.

### 5. Earned Trust in AI Code (67)
Tieto's rubric (Clarity 30, Practicality 25, Measurement 25, Evidence 20; L241-255) rewards exactly what most teams skip: a seeded-defect experiment against a baseline, tested abstention and a SHA-bound owner sign-off. The provided branches give unusually concrete demo material (opportunity.md). It sits at fifth because density is the worst of the top group: developer teams will find the topic natural, the competitive report estimates medium-high crowding, and the concept weighting (L151) narrows the gap between strong engineers and good presenters. There is also no feasibility report; I estimate feasibility at 8 because the build is pure software on a provided repo, but the C# codebase is a mild friction. **What would change it:** a low on-site team count, or Tieto mentors confirming that measured experiments outrank concept polish, would move it into the top three.

### 6. Break the Agent (67)
The rubric is rigorous and objective (judges rerun the evaluation and hunt for exploits; L449-451, L503-517). Rigour suits an ML/eval team, and many teams will lose points on fairness or reward integrity. The tiebreaker of improving a small model (L453) is a lever few will reach. Feasibility is the drag. Finding a reproducible, fair gap is research risk with a decision gate around hour 14. Reward isolation from a shell-capable OpenCode agent and Harbor bugs add engineering risk, and it is unknown whether a scripted reference policy counts as the "stronger reference run". **What would change it:** if mentors confirm scripted references are accepted, and a quick pilot shows a frontier agent failing reliably on our chosen mechanic (for example budgeted experimentation), it rises to about 72. If the pilot shows no stable gap by the gate, it is not worth entering.

### Just outside: Spread, AI Glasses, Web Real-Time Database (66)
Spread has the best pure skill fit of any challenge, and its jury explicitly rewards elegance and ablations. But it is a two-stage lottery: about 50-65% to make the top 10 of about 64 teams (spread/feasibility.md), then roughly 1 in 3 to 1 in 4 for first place (estimate), so about 15-20% overall. Ranking rounds have also already started. Strong practice-leaderboard results early on Saturday would move it to the top tier. AI Glasses depends on getting time on 20 shared devices; guaranteed device allocation would lift it to about 70.

## Dependence on competition estimates
The gaps between places 1 and 10 (75 to 66) are smaller than the plausible error in Density, which accounts for 20% and is unsourced for every challenge except Spread. A walk around the sponsor booths on Friday night to count teams per challenge is the cheapest evidence that could reorder the top ten.
