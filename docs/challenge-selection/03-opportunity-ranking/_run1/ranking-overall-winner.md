# Ranking for objective "overall-winner"

**Summary**
- Objective: the chance that a project built for this challenge gets into the peer-selected top 5 and then wins the seven-person jury vote. Partner prizes are ignored.
- Top six (scores are estimates on a 0-100 relative scale, not probabilities): **AI Glasses (Meta) 65**, **Internet Butler (Exa) 61**, **Make Blueprints Talk (Stoa) 60**, **Earned Trust in AI Code (Tieto) 60**, **Decode the Noise (Hostinger) 60**, **Personal Economic Trainer (S-Bank) 59**. Ranks 2 to 9 sit within 4 points, so the idea and execution matter more than the challenge choice in that range.
- AI Glasses leads because the peer floor is the gate: something worn on the face at the table is understood in seconds. Its score depends on getting reliable time on one of the 20 shared devices (CHALLENGES.md:L914-915), and our feasibility report for it is an unfinished draft.
- Hardware-run challenges (Intel arm, Rokoko gloves, Arduino, Aalto humanoid) have the most wow but carry heavy feasibility penalties. Simulation and benchmark challenges (Spread, Break the Agent) are rigorous but hard to read at a table.
- Trusted Trade Agents and Valio Aimo are **the same challenge** (Valio Aimo, CHALLENGES.md:L547-693) researched twice, and they get the same score.

## Method

### Criteria and weights
The six-criterion jury rubric from `00-rules-and-jury.md` §7 (the weights are the digest's own estimate), each scored 0-5:

| # | Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | Worthwhile problem, specific user | 20% |
| C2 | Distinctive insight and technical depth | 20% |
| C3 | Working end-to-end live demo | 15% |
| C4 | Honest measured evidence (baseline, failures, metric that can't be gamed) | 15% |
| C5 | Peer-floor legibility and wow (understood in 60-90 s) | 15% |
| C6 | Credible path forward | 15% |

**Score = Rubric% × F × G**, where:
- **Rubric%** = weighted sum of C1-C6 / 5 × 100. It is scored for the *best direction identified in the opportunity report*, not the obvious idea.
- **F (feasibility and team fit, 0.6-1.0)** covers buildability in about 38.5 h, demo reliability, external gates (device or station allocation, licences, organiser-run finals) and a penalty for physical work in proportion to the effort involved. Consumer devices are not penalised for being hardware, only for access risk. Missing or partial research reports cost about 0.05.
- **G (peer-review gate, 0.7 + 0.06 × C5)** applies because the jury only sees the five finalists that peers pick (`00-rules-and-jury.md` §3, §7). Legibility therefore counts twice: once as a jury criterion and once as the gate. With C5 = 5, G = 1.00; with C5 = 2, G = 0.82.

Calibration notes: the research agents' 1-10 numbers (demo_potential, jury_fit and so on) were used only as inputs. I opened the underlying reports where a placement depended on them: the Aaltonomous feasibility report (real-robot run out of our hands, about 4/10 to reach and sort well), the AI Glasses and Green European Renaissance feasibility reports (both unfinished drafts), and the rules and jury digest. Competition levels are estimates from the competitive reports. Peer review is probably cross-track (unknown, `00-rules-and-jury.md` §3), so crowding *within* a challenge mainly hurts through "looks like five other tables" (C2, C5) rather than directly.

## Top six in detail

**1. AI Glasses (65).** It is the only challenge where the demo *is* the pitch: a peer puts the glasses on, or watches us use them, and gets the value in seconds. That is why C5 = 5 and the gate factor is 1.0. Meta's own rubric (four equal parts: use case, execution, real-world value, UX/trust; CHALLENGES.md:L934-937) overlaps heavily with the jury's, so one build serves both. The opportunity report found that Meta's accessibility showcase covers only blind/low-vision users, which leaves Deaf/HoH, motor and cognitive-load audiences open. Pairing one of those with on-phone privacy redaction as a measurable feature gives a named user plus evidence. It loses points on C4 (measuring is harder with live perception) and on F: the 20 shared devices have an unknown allocation (L914-915), Web Apps have no camera or mic (Meta docs, cited in feasibility draft), and the feasibility report never finished. **Would change it:** confirmation on site that we can hold a device for most of the build (up) or only Mock Device Kit time (down to about 50, below the software cluster); a finished feasibility check showing DAT streaming latency is usable for the chosen interaction.

**2. Internet Butler (61).** A tedious real errand ("is my rent increase legal under the law that changed on 1 Oct 2026?") is the most relatable problem in the list, and the before/after against a bare LLM giving the stale rule is easy to show and measure. Pure software, so demo reliability is high once judge-typed queries are handled. It is held back by saturation: the competitive report rates it high, with many Exa wrapper winners and vertical claim tools, so C2 is only 2.5 and peers may see several look-alikes. **Would change it:** a test showing Exa indexes finlex.fi and kela.fi well enough for a reliable live Finnish case (up); finding on site that many teams chose the same rent or benefits angle (down).

**3. Make Blueprints Talk (60).** It has the highest raw rubric score (80) of any challenge. The insight is concrete and checkable: exact counts and areas parsed from vector, DXF and IFC sources, shown as overlays, against VLMs that score 0.40-0.55 on door and window counting (AECV-Bench, cited in the research). The feasibility spike already matched 7/7 IFC counts and 5/5 room areas. The dataset of about 120k Q&A pairs gives held-out metrics with no fabrication. Stoa is a Lifeline-backed company and Timo is a juror (indirect link). It ranks third, not first, because a construction drawing is less striking to a peer than a consumer product (C5 = 3), and 67% of public questions are raster counts that need a trained detector. **Would change it:** Stoa confirming the evaluation protocol and that training on CubiCasa annotations is allowed (up); a test showing the overlay demo makes non-construction people say "oh" within a minute (up to about 65).

**4. Earned Trust in AI Code (60).** Every hacker at Junction has reviewed an agent-written PR, so "this 237-file diff is 222 snapshot files that reduce to 3 distinct lines, plus one real anomaly" is a legible moment for this specific audience. The seeded-defect experiment with catch rate, false positives and human minutes against a baseline fits Daksh, Beer and Arturs well. It ranks below Blueprints because there is no feasibility report (F = 0.85), AI code review is a crowded product category (CodeRabbit, Qodo and others), and Tieto's rubric rewards the concept over the build (CHALLENGES.md:L151), which may pull effort away from the demo. **Would change it:** a feasibility check that the C# branches build and test cleanly in a sandbox (up); seeing many review-bot entries in the general pool (down).

**5. Decode the Noise (60).** A reliable build (feasibility 9/10) with a strong evidence story: a falsifiable eval page reporting precision, false-positive rate and abstain rate against a user-agent-regex baseline, plus the novel three-way triage of humans, legitimate AI agents and abusive bots. A live moment ("we attack our own site and watch the firewall rule appear") is possible. It is held back on C1 and C6: the user is a small-site operator, which is a real but unexciting persona, and the propaganda framing carries ethics and data-licence risks. **Would change it:** Hostinger sharing real abuse logs (up through C4 and C1); judges treating traffic-bot detection as off-theme relative to propaganda (down, partner-side mainly).

**6. Personal Economic Trainer (59).** "Chat with your own money" plus "safe to spend this week" is instantly understood, and S-mobiili's 2.6M users give a concrete path (C6 = 4). The best direction (deterministic money engine, a receipt behind every number, a golden-set evaluation against a naive LLM) gives honest evidence. It ranks sixth because the insight is not distinctive (C2 = 2.5): banks and fintechs already ship assistants, the data is synthetic, and peers will likely see other finance chatbots. The competitive report is missing, so crowding is an estimate. **Would change it:** a striking insight from the 5,100-customer data, such as a backtested shock buffer that would have prevented the observed overdrafts in 247 of 361 sampled customers, shown as a felt "replay your year" before/after (up to the low 60s).

## Caveats
- Every score is an estimate. The gaps between ranks 2 and 11 (61 to 56) are smaller than the uncertainty in any single criterion, so treat them as a tier rather than an order.
- Unknowns that could move many rows at once: whether peer review is cross-track or within-track, the top-5 pitch format (live demo vs slides), and whether one project may enter several main challenges (`00-rules-and-jury.md` §9).
- Reports missing or unfinished: feasibility for ai-glasses (draft), green-european-renaissance (draft), earned-trust-in-ai-code, rethinking-recruitment; competitive for valio-aimo, rethinking-recruitment, personal-economic-trainer.
