# Ranking for objective "challenge-prize": probability of winning a partner award

**Summary**
- **Decode the Noise (Hostinger) scores highest, at 75.** Measured detection quality is 40% of its score (CHALLENGES.md:L76). That rewards an ML team with an evaluation harness over the many LLM "is this propaganda" wrappers. Web-log bot detection ("Clean the traffic") is an officially listed direction (CHALLENGES.md:L35), which removes the main doubt raised in an earlier draft.
- **Trusted Trade Agents (Valio Aimo) scores 74 and is effectively tied for first.** `valio-aimo` and `trusted-trade-agents` are **the same challenge**: the "Valio Aimo®" heading at CHALLENGES.md:L575 is a subsection of "Trusted Trade Agents" (L535), and both have one rubric and one prize table (L659-693). Next come **Wikipedia Through Time (73)**, **Make Blueprints Talk (72)** and **Earned Trust in AI Code (69)**.
- Places 1 to about 9 are separated by less than the error in our competition estimates. Team counts per challenge are **unknown** everywhere except Spread, which had about 64 teams registered at level 8 on Friday evening (02-research/spread/feasibility.md).
- The physical-robotics and embedded challenges (Intel, Rokoko, Aalto humanoid, Arduino) rank last. They have smaller fields, but robot or kit time and an unrehearsable live demo outweigh that for this team.

All scores and per-criterion numbers are **my estimates**. They are built from the reports under `01-challenge-intelligence/` and `02-research/`, with challenge facts cited by line number. An earlier, interrupted draft (`_run1/ranking-challenge-prize.md`) was written before the feasibility reports for Earned Trust, AI Glasses, Aalto, Recruitment and S-Pankki existed. I used its sound structure but rescored every challenge.

## Criteria and weights

Each criterion is scored 0-10, multiplied by its weight and summed to a score out of 100. I then adjusted the sum by at most ±2 for risks the criteria do not capture, and say so in the rationale. Overall-jury appeal is ignored, as the objective requires.

| Criterion | Weight | What a 10 means |
|---|---|---|
| **Fit**: what the sponsor scores matches what we build exceptionally well | 25% | The rubric rewards full-stack, agent, ML/eval or mobile depth, and needs no embedded or robotics skill |
| **Density** (inverse): competition from Junction teams | 20% | Few teams are expected, or a hard filter (gate, difficulty, kit count) thins the field. **Estimates only**, except Spread |
| **Differentiation**: room to beat the obvious entries | 20% | The opportunity and competitive reports show clear, evidenced gaps that the obvious builds will miss |
| **Feasibility and demo reliability**, including the hardware penalty | 20% | Comfortably buildable in 40 h, with a deterministic, rehearsable judged demo. Reduced in proportion to physical work and to dependence on shared kits or sponsor-run hardware |
| **Showcase**: how visibly an entry can exercise the sponsor's technology or data | 15% | The sponsor's tech is central, visibly exercised, and the sponsor scores its use |

Calibration notes:
- The research agents' 1-10 numbers were inputs only. I re-checked the underlying reports wherever a placement depended on them: Spread's gate and team count, Decode the Noise's scope, the Valio duplicate, the Tieto and Stoa rubrics, the MariaDB rubric, and the Meta prize table.
- Prize size is not a criterion, because the objective is probability, not expected value. A larger prize may attract more teams, and I folded that into Density.
- "Win" means first place. Every challenge except AI Glasses also has 2nd and 3rd prizes. AI Glasses has a single "Best overall" prize (CHALLENGES.md:L939-943), so its podium odds are lower than its score suggests.

## Top six in detail

### 1. Decode the Noise (75)
The rubric is the most quantitative of the software challenges. Detection quality is 40% and is defined as accuracy, false-positive handling and explainability (L76). The brief asks outright for precision and false-positive rates (L43) and says the sponsor cares more about honest detection logic than a polished front end (L29). That rewards a labelled evaluation against a baseline, an abstain band and evidence-linked explanations. Most obvious entries (LLM-only classifiers, fake-news checkers) will skip those (decode-the-noise/competitive.md). The mandatory live Hostinger deployment with a public URL (L29) is routine for us and filters out weaker teams. A "Clean the traffic" detector that enforces blocks through the Hostinger VPS firewall API also shows off the sponsor's own infrastructure, which the opportunity report found unused. An earlier draft worried that traffic-bot detection might be off-theme. L35 lists it as an official direction, so that worry is gone. **What would change it:** it drops into the low 70s if the VPS or domain arrives late Saturday, or if AI Router credits turn out tiny. It also drops if many strong ML teams pick it; density is an estimate. A mentor saying they favour text-propaganda detection would hurt too, since the PTC corpus is academic-use only. It is no longer a toss-up with Valio if mentors confirm that a firewall-enforced, measured traffic detector is what Hostinger hoped for.

### 2. Trusted Trade Agents / Valio Aimo (74)
This challenge rewards the shape of system we build fastest: an agent, a deterministic core, a policy engine, approvals, an audit log and exception handling, all graded on one end-to-end flow in a mocked environment (L659-679). Both research tracks verified the mock API live. They also found traps that the obvious "MCP + chatbot + approve button" entries will miss: unsafe substitutions, sentinel stock values, past cut-offs, and lactose-free milk still carrying the milk allergen. That evidence maps onto robustness (15%) and controls (20%). It ranks a point behind Decode the Noise on density alone. €4k is the largest Agentic-track cash prize, the valio-aimo competitive report expects high interest, and 2025 entrants exist (github.com/pasiones/Hackathon2025, cited in that report), so the field is probably wide and partly experienced. **What would change it:** an on-site sign of a small field, or Valio confirming they value AP2/UCP-style open mandates and a seller-side gateway, would put it first. An official order endpoint arriving would commoditise our order layer and pull it down, and so would a booth crowded with teams.

### 3. Wikipedia Through Time (73)
This has the best sponsor showcase of any challenge. MariaDB use is scored directly (20%, L1983), and combining vector RAG with system-versioned tables is left undone by the sponsor's own starters. A time-aware RAG that answers "as of date X" and cites the edit that changed the answer is therefore both differentiated and exactly what the sponsor wants to blog about. Feasibility is good: the feasibility report estimates about 95% for a minimal version and 70-80% for the strong combined version. Implementation and scalability across S/M/L datasets is 20% (L1987-1989), and that is the risk. Loading M is unwritten upstream, vector inserts are slow, and a vector index cannot sit on a partitioned SVT table. **What would change it:** mentors accepting a subset of M with extrapolated numbers, or supplying a tested loader, would raise it to about 75. Discovering that many teams are doing combined RAG+SVT, or that a vector index cannot coexist with SVT at all, would drop it to the high 60s.

### 4. Make Blueprints Talk (72)
This is the ML-heaviest software challenge and has the best evidence that our approach works. The feasibility spike already answered about a third of the public questions exactly and deterministically, with overlays that come free from the same computation. That targets accuracy, verification and cost, three of the four 25% criteria (L1607-1610). Its difficulty (7) and €1k prize should keep chat-wrapper teams from competing seriously (estimate). Two things hold it back. First, 67% of questions are raster CubiCasa symbol counts that need a trained detector, and training on the public CubiCasa annotations may count as leakage. Second, the evaluation protocol and cost metric are unknown, so we cannot be sure our deterministic advantage is what gets measured. **What would change it:** Stoa confirming a hidden test set that includes IFC, DXF and vector sources, or allowing CubiCasa training, could make it first. A raster-only hidden set with tight cost scoring would drop it to the mid-60s.

### 5. Earned Trust in AI Code (69)
Tieto scores Clarity 30%, Practicality 25%, Measurement 25% and Evidence 20% (L241-255). The measured parts reward what most teams will only claim: a seeded-defect experiment against a baseline, abstentions that are tested, and a SHA-bound owner sign-off. The sample branches give unusually concrete material: 222 of 237 files in #2297 are mechanical snapshots, and human PR #2305 is an independent oracle (earned-trust-in-ai-code/opportunity.md). Feasibility is now backed by a report (9/10 buildable), with only one hour-1 risk, the EOL .NET 8 build. It sits fifth because AI code review is a developer's daily pain, so interest is likely high (competitive.md estimates medium-high crowding). The concept-heavy weighting also lets good presenters close the gap, and there is little sponsor technology to show off. **What would change it:** a low team count, or Tieto mentors saying measured experiments outrank concept polish, would move it into the top three. Many teams at the Tieto booth would push it below Break the Agent.

### 6. Break the Agent (67)
The rubric is objective: judges rerun the reference run, three agent runs and two baselines, then hunt for exploits for 15 minutes (L449-451, L503-517). Rigour suits an ML/eval team, and many entries will lose points on fairness (withheld information scores 0) or reward integrity. The small-model tiebreaker (L453) is a lever few teams will reach. Feasibility is the drag. Finding a fair, reproducible gap against a frontier agent is research risk with a go/no-go gate around hour 14. A shell-capable OpenCode agent can probe the reward, and known Harbor issues can turn trials into false failures. Whether a scripted reference policy counts as the "stronger reference run" is unknown. **What would change it:** mentors accepting a scripted reference, plus a quick pilot showing a frontier agent failing reliably on our mechanic (for example budgeted experimentation), would lift it to about 72. No stable gap by the gate means abandoning it.

### Just outside the top six
- **Web Real-Time Database (66)** is easy for us but has the most commodity idea space.
- **AI Glasses (65)** has a strong fit and showcase, but rests on getting time on 20 shared devices for a single prize. Guaranteed device allocation would lift it to about 70.
- **Spread (63)** has the best pure skill fit of all. But about 64 registered teams and a one-week top-10 gate make first place roughly 10-15% (my estimate: 50-65% to pass the gate times about 1 in 4-6 among finalists). Ranking rounds have also been running since Friday 19:30. A top-5 practice-leaderboard position by Saturday morning would move it into the top tier.

## How much the ranking depends on competition estimates
The gap from 1st (75) to 9th (65) is smaller than the plausible error in Density, a 20% weight with no sourced team counts except Spread's. Swapping Density between Decode the Noise and Valio would reverse their order, and the same goes for Wikipedia and Blueprints. The cheapest evidence that could reorder the top ten is a count of teams at the Hostinger, Valio, MariaDB, Stoa and Tieto booths (or on Discord) on Friday night, plus answers to each top-five challenge's main open question: Hostinger VPS timing and Router credits, Valio order format, the MariaDB scalability bar, Stoa's evaluation protocol, and Tieto's weighting of measurement.
