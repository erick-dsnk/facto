# Ranking for objective "overall-winner"

**Summary**
- Objective: the chance that a project built for a challenge is picked by peers for the overall top 5 and then wins the seven-person jury vote. Partner prizes are ignored. Scores are relative estimates on a 0-100 scale, not probabilities.
- Top six: **AI Glasses (Meta) 65**, **Earned Trust in AI Code (Tieto) 64**, **Make Blueprints Talk (Stoa) 62**, **Rethinking Recruitment (Espoo) 61** and **Internet Butler (Exa) 61** (tied), **Decode the Noise (Hostinger) 60**. Ranks 1-11 span only 65 to 55, which is less than the uncertainty in any single criterion. Within that band the idea and its execution matter more than which challenge we pick.
- AI Glasses leads only if we can get dependable time on one of the 20 shared devices (CHALLENGES.md:L914-915). If we can't, it falls to about 45-50. Earned Trust is the strongest pure-software option: a "237 files becomes 13 to read" before/after that every hacker understands, backed by a seeded-defect measurement.
- Challenges that depend on physical robots or boards (Intel, Rokoko, Arduino, Aalto humanoid) have floor appeal but are heavily penalised on feasibility. Simulation or benchmark challenges (Spread, Break the Agent) support rigorous evidence but are hard to read at a peer table.
- Trusted Trade Agents and Valio Aimo are **the same challenge**: one brief at CHALLENGES.md:L535-693 with Valio Aimo as the sponsor section (L575) and a single prize table (L681-693). It was researched twice, so both rows get the same score.

## Method

### Criteria and weights
These are the six jury criteria from `00-rules-and-jury.md` §7. The weights are the digest's own estimate. Each criterion is scored 0-5 for the **best direction found in that challenge's opportunity report**, not for the obvious idea.

| # | Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | Worthwhile problem, specific user | 20% |
| C2 | Distinctive insight and technical depth (vs the obvious approach and existing products) | 20% |
| C3 | Working end-to-end live demo | 15% |
| C4 | Honest measured evidence (baseline, failure cases, a metric that can't be gamed) | 15% |
| C5 | Peer-floor legibility and wow (understood in 60-90 s) | 15% |
| C6 | Credible path forward (next user, reason to return, bounded scale step) | 15% |

**Score = Rubric% × F × G**
- **Rubric%** is the weighted sum of C1-C6, divided by 5 and multiplied by 100.
- **F (feasibility and team fit, 0.60-1.00)** combines several things: whether it can be built in about 38.5 h, how reliable the demo will be, and external gates (device or station allocation, licence approval, organiser-run finals, API limits). Physical hardware work is penalised in proportion to how much of it the challenge needs. A consumer device (Meta glasses) is penalised only for access risk, not for being hardware.
- **G (peer-review gate) = 0.70 + 0.06 × C5.** The jury sees only the five projects that peers pick (`00-rules-and-jury.md` §3, §7), so legibility counts twice: once as a jury criterion and once as the gate. C5 = 5 gives G = 1.00; C5 = 2 gives G = 0.82.
- Tie-break, following the digest: if C3 or C5 scores 1 or lower, the overall chance is treated as near zero. No challenge falls that low on the best direction found.

### Calibration
- The research agents' 1-10 numbers were used only as inputs. They were written by different agents with different calibrations. Where a placement depended on one, I reopened the underlying report:
  - ai-glasses/feasibility.md: build 7/10; demo 6/10, dropping to about 3/10 without dependable registered glasses; go/no-go decided at the Meta booth in the first hours.
  - earned-trust-in-ai-code/opportunity.md: the agent rated overall-jury fit at 5/10 and noted that past overall winners were products or hardware rather than dev tools. I scored C5 higher than that agent did, because the peer reviewers are hackers who have read agent-written PRs. That is an inference, and it is the main reason this row ranks high.
  - make-blueprints-talk/opportunity.md and feasibility.md: the preview spike got 7/7 IFC counts and 5/5 room areas exactly, but 67% of questions are raster counts that need a trained detector.
  - personal-economic-trainer/opportunity.md (jury readings per direction).
  - internet-butler/feasibility.md: Exa's coverage of Finnish official sites is unknown (L85, L120).
- A previous run of this ranking (`_run1/ranking-overall-winner.md`) was written while four reports were still missing or unfinished. This run uses the now-complete feasibility reports for ai-glasses, earned-trust-in-ai-code, rethinking-recruitment and green-european-renaissance. The main changes are that Earned Trust and Rethinking Recruitment move up (F rises from 0.85-0.90 to 0.95).
- **Competition is uncertain.** All crowding levels are estimates from the competitive reports, and team counts per challenge are unknown. Whether peer review is cross-track is also unknown (`00-rules-and-jury.md` §3). Crowding therefore enters only through C2 and C5, as "looks like other tables". Rows whose placement depends on a crowding estimate are marked below.
- **Sponsor-linked jurors** (Daksh for AfterQuery, Beer for Context.dev, Arturs for Verda, Timo for Lifeline/Stoa; `00-rules-and-jury.md` §5) are **not** scored as an advantage. The PDF warns against predicting votes, and recusal is unknown.

## Top six in detail

**1. AI Glasses (65).** This is the only challenge where the demo *is* the pitch. A peer puts the glasses on, or watches us use them hands-free, and gets the value in seconds, so C5 = 5 and the gate factor is 1.0. Meta's rubric (use case, execution, real-world value, UX and trust at 25% each; CHALLENGES.md:L934-937) overlaps heavily with the jury's, and its explicit robustness and consent requirements (L900-906) push us toward measurable engineering rather than a see-and-ask wrapper. The opportunity report found that Meta's accessibility showcase centres on blind and low-vision users, which leaves Deaf/HoH, motor and cognitive-load users open. Pairing one of those audiences with on-phone privacy redaction gives a named user and something to measure. The row loses points on C4 (live perception is harder to benchmark) and on F. The feasibility report puts build at 7/10 and demo reliability at 6/10. About 70-80% of the build can run on Mock Device Kit, but the demo needs one of the 20 shared units (L914-915). Each pair supports one Meta account and one registered Developer Mode app, and per-account capability gating applies, with Finland support unknown. **What would change it:** if the Meta booth confirms early that we can hold a registered, capability-enabled device through judging, the score rises to about 70. If only mock or brief access is possible, demo reliability is about 3/10 and the score drops to about 45-50, below the software cluster.

**2. Earned Trust in AI Code (64).** The best pure-software fit for the jury. The before/after comes from the sponsor's own branches: on ju26-issue-2297, 222 of 237 files are snapshot files that reduce to 3 repeated lines plus 1 anomaly. That reads in about 30 seconds to a hacker audience. A seeded-defect experiment (catch rate, false positives and owner minutes against a baseline) and fail-before/pass-after tests give the strongest measured evidence outside Break the Agent, which serves Daksh, Beer and Arturs. Feasibility is now confirmed at 9/8 (one .NET 8 Docker build risk, to be solved in hour 1). It ranks below Glasses because the opportunity agent rated overall-jury fit at only 5/10. AI code review is a crowded product category, past overall winners were products or hardware rather than dev tools (competitive report), and Tieto weights the concept over the implementation (L151). **What would change it:** seeing many review-bot projects in the general pool would lower it to about 58, because this placement depends on a crowding estimate. A hallway test where non-Tieto hackers instantly "get" the reading plan would confirm it. If the .NET build fails in hour 1, it drops sharply.

**3. Make Blueprints Talk (62).** The highest raw rubric score (80) of any challenge. The insight is concrete and checkable: "the file already contains the answer, and the AI was looking at a picture of it". Exact counts and areas parsed from IFC, DXF and vector PDF are shown as numbered overlays a visitor can click to correct, against VLMs that score 0.40-0.55 on symbol counting. The feasibility spike already matched 7/7 IFC counts, 5/5 room areas and 65/65 DXF window counts, and the roughly 120k labelled Q&A allow held-out metrics without fabrication. It ranks third because construction drawings move peers less than consumer products do (C5 = 3), and because 67% of preview questions are raster counts that need a trained detector, which is the riskiest workstream (F = 0.88). **What would change it:** Stoa confirming its evaluation protocol and allowing CubiCasa training would raise it. A table test where non-construction people react to the click-to-correct overlay within a minute would raise it to about 66. Learning that judging uses only unseen raster scans would lower it.

**4= Rethinking Recruitment (61).** The problem is felt by many people in the room: AI-polished CVs bury real capability, and companies get flooded with irrelevant approaches (L1764-1784). Espoo offers a concrete pilot and a presentation to its city network (L1822-1824), which gives C1 and C6 a 4. The best direction (company need-elicitation interview, then candidate voice evidence answering the company's own tasks, then mutual opt-in suggestions with no ranking, plus a counterfactual synthetic-twin fairness test) is buildable at 9/8. It ties for fourth rather than placing higher because technical depth is modest (C2 = 2.5), and it could read as a Brella clone or as profiling, which L1806 prohibits. **What would change it:** a fairness metric that produces a striking result, for example showing that a naive LLM matcher shifts outcomes by nationality and ours does not, would raise C2 and C4. Espoo saying that mutual-opt-in suggestions still count as profiling would lower it.

**4= Internet Butler (61).** "Is my rent increase legal under the law that changed on 1 Oct 2026?" is among the most relatable problems in the set. The bare-LLM-gives-the-stale-rule versus cited-current-rule comparison is an easy, measurable before/after, and the drafted letter awaiting approval is a visible end state (L709). It is pure software at 9/7. It is held back by saturation: the competitive report rates it high, with general agents, vertical claim tools and Exa's own demo gallery. That gives C2 = 2.5, and peers may see look-alikes. The judges' live queries are also a reliability hazard (L757). **What would change it:** an hour-1 test showing that Exa indexes finlex.fi and kela.fi well enough for a reliable Finnish case would confirm it, and a strong temporal-correctness eval would raise it to about 64. Seeing several rent or benefits agents on the floor would lower it (crowding-dependent).

**6. Decode the Noise (60).** It is a reliable build (9/8) with a strong evidence story: a public "try to fool it" eval page reporting precision, false-positive rate and abstain rate against a user-agent-regex baseline. The three-way split between humans, legitimate AI agents (which send a stock Chrome user agent) and abusive bots is new, and enforcing it through the Hostinger firewall API gives a live moment: "we attack our own site and watch the rule appear". It ranks sixth on C1 and C6, because a small-VPS owner is a real but unexciting persona and the propaganda framing carries data-licence and naming risks. **What would change it:** if Hostinger shares real abuse logs, C1 and C4 rise to about 64. If the floor treats it as "another fake-news detector", or the AI Router credits run out mid-demo, it falls.

## Caveats
- Every score is an estimate. Ranks 1-11 lie within 10 points, so treat them as two tiers: 60-65 (ranks 1-6) and 55-59 (ranks 7-11). They are not a strict order.
- Unknowns that could move many rows at once (`00-rules-and-jury.md` §9):
  - whether peer review is cross-track;
  - the top-5 pitch format (in 2024 it was slides-only for 3 minutes, which would weaken live-demo advantages such as Glasses);
  - whether one project may enter several main challenges.
- Competition levels are estimates and team counts are unknown. Rows 2, 4= (Butler) and 7 are the most sensitive to them.
